<section class="page-lead">
  <p class="item-kicker">Research program</p>
  <h1>Dynamic water risk in a changing Earth system</h1>
  <p>Water risk is produced event by event as storms meet soils, rivers, cities, infrastructure, and people. My research asks how changes in climate forcing and landscape state move through the water cycle to become hazards, and what evidence is needed to predict those hazards before their consequences are fully visible.</p>
</section>

    <h2>A connected research trajectory</h2>
    <p>I began with urban drainage, nature-based systems, water quality, and real-time control. This work established a base in physical modeling, intervention design, and field measurement. I then developed open models for floods, rivers, reservoirs, and dam-breaks, including HydroPol2D and HydroHP.</p>
    <p>My recent work links these models to rainfall timing, hydrologic state, satellite observations, flood reconstruction, and human exposure. The scale has changed from individual drainage systems to cities, regions, and national datasets, but the central question remains the same: which processes and observations are needed to explain changing water risk?</p>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="research-thrust">
  <p class="research-thrust__number">01</p>
  <div><h2>Explain how forcing and landscape state generate water extremes</h2><p>The timing of rain and the condition of the land before a storm can change runoff as much as total rainfall. I study how rainfall structure, soil wetness, vegetation, terrain, land cover, and infrastructure control the shift from storage to rapid flow and inundation. Work in Brazil provides long records and methodological depth, while India and California provide contrasting tests of what transfers across climates.</p><p>Current outputs include national rainfall datasets, empirical storm-timing curves, and tests of when historical design assumptions no longer describe present conditions.</p></div>
</section>

<section class="research-thrust">
  <p class="research-thrust__number">02</p>
  <div><h2>Observe, reconstruct, and predict surface-water extremes</h2><p>I combine satellite flood extent, terrain, river geometry, stream measurements, and physical models to reconstruct past events and test flood predictions. Urban floods, river floods, and dam-breaks are different tests of the same scientific problem: when is a simple representation sufficient, and when does omitted physics change the answer?</p><p>HydroPol2D, LIMIAR, and related open tools support this work. The goal is not one universal model. It is prediction whose assumptions, uncertainty, and limits can be examined.</p></div>
</section>

<section class="research-thrust">
  <p class="research-thrust__number">03</p>
  <div><h2>Trace hazards into consequences and test adaptation</h2><p>Water depth alone does not define risk. I connect physical hazard with infrastructure, population, socioeconomic, and health information to separate changes in the hazard from changes in who and what lie in its path. My current Stanford work reconstructs flood exposure and investigates health outcomes in California and India with domain specialists.</p><p>This work also examines nature-based drainage, structural measures, and operational control. Regional multi-hazard analysis provides context where floods overlap with drought, wildfire, and social vulnerability without treating all hazards as interchangeable.</p></div>
</section>
